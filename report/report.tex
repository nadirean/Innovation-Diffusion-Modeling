\begin{abstract}
Modeling and simulation of a one-dimensional reaction--diffusion system comparing classical solvers, sensitivity analysis, data assimilation, PINN and SuperNet methods, with key metrics and runtimes. Main findings: PINNs perform well with sparse measurements and SuperNet improves robustness to parameter uncertainty.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Problem Statement}
Reaction--diffusion equations combine local kinetics with spatial transport and model many biological and chemical systems.

\subsection{Mathematical Model}
The main object of study in the project is a one-dimensional parabolic equation:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = D \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + R(u), \quad x \in [0, 1], \ t \in [0, T]
\end{equation}
where $u(x,t)$ represents a density or concentration of a substance. The reaction term $R(u)$ is nonlinear and given by:
\begin{equation}
    R(u) = (p + q u)(1 - u)
\end{equation}
Parameters $D, p, q$ control diffusion strength, the baseline growth rate, and the nonlinear autocatalytic effect, respectively.

\subsection{Boundary and Initial Conditions}
To ensure uniqueness of the solution and to model an isolated system, homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions were used:
\begin{equation}
    \left. \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \right|_{x=0} = 0, \quad \left. \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \right|_{x=1} = 0
\end{equation}
The initial condition $u(x,0)$ was specified in three variants:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{gaussian}: Gaussian centered at $0.5$,
    \item \texttt{localized}: rectangular pulse in the center,
    \item \texttt{small\_random}: small random perturbations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Task 3: Inference and NN Models}
Prediction-time tests show the ODE (RK45) costs ~1.29 ms, while a simple feed-forward NN predicts in ~0.87 ms (~30\% faster), indicating NNs' potential for real-time use.

\section{Task 4: Sensitivity Analysis (SA)}
An analysis of the impact of parameters $D,p,q$ on system behavior was conducted.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Morris}: fast screening; $q$ (autocatalysis) has the largest nonlinear effect on the final profile.
    \item \textbf{Sobol}: variance-based analysis confirming $D$ smooths the profile and $p,q$ interactions affect bifurcation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Task 5: Data Assimilation (DA)}
This task concerned reconstructing the system state from noisy measurements.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ABC}: likelihood-free posterior estimation for $p,q$ — stable but computationally expensive.
    \item \textbf{3D-Var}: variational method; faster than ABC and provides good field reconstruction even at low SNR.
\end{itemize}

\section{Task 6: PINN -- Physics-Informed Neural Networks}
\subsection{Architecture and Training Procedure}
The PINN is an MLP with 5 layers of 64 neurons and residual connections; \texttt{Tanh} activations ensure smooth derivatives for PDE residual evaluation.

\subsection{Loss Function}
The key component of a PINN is a composite loss function:
\begin{equation}
    L = L_{data} + \lambda_{phy} L_{phy} + \lambda_{bc} L_{bc}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $L_{data} = \text{MSE}$ on sparse measurement points (data assimilation).
    \item $L_{phy} = \frac{1}{N_c} \sum |u_t - D u_{xx} - R(u)|^2$ computed at collocation points using \texttt{autograd}.
    \item $L_{bc}$ enforces Neumann boundary conditions.
\end{itemize}
A \textbf{Dynamic Weight Scheduling} increased $\lambda_{phy}$ during training (0.1 → 2.0) so the network first fits data then emphasizes physics.

\paragraph{Note on boundary-condition experiment.}
As an additional experiment, we tried to enforce the Neumann (no-flux) boundary conditions more aggressively by explicitly sampling boundary points ($x=0$ and $x=1$ for random $t$) and adding a dedicated boundary penalty based on $u_x(0,t)$ and $u_x(1,t)$ computed via \texttt{autograd}. In practice this resulted in a total failure of training: boundary derivatives at the exact domain edges were extremely noisy/ill-conditioned early on, so when the boundary term was weighted strongly the optimization became unstable (occasionally producing exploding gradients / NaNs), and when weighted moderately it pushed the network towards a trivial overly-smooth (almost constant) solution that satisfied $u_x\approx 0$ everywhere but did not match the reaction dynamics.
For the final reported run we therefore kept the boundary enforcement weak and relied primarily on the physics residual loss and the fact that the reference PDE data are generated with a Neumann Laplacian.

\section{Task 7: SuperNet and Ensemble Modeling}
\subsection{SuperModel Concept}
Task 7 considers parameter uncertainty: the SuperModel ODE is an ensemble coupled by
\begin{equation}
    \dot{u}_i = f(u_i; \theta_i) + C(\bar{u} - u_i)
\end{equation}
with ensemble mean $\bar{u}$, yielding more stable predictions than single models.

\subsection{SuperNet PINN}
A \textbf{SuperNet PINN} was implemented to learn to represent the entire family of solutions. The network shows strong robustness to input parameter perturbations due to its ability to capture the general structure of the differential operator.

\section{Discussion}
Key observations:
\begin{itemize}
\item Explicit methods are fast but CFL-limited; implicit schemes allow larger time steps at modest extra cost.
\item PINNs are competitive (MAE~0.03) but incur higher training cost; SuperNet reduces error and increases robustness.
\item Method choice depends on the use case: lightweight NNs for fast inference, PINN/SuperNet for parameter-flexible accuracy.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}
The project highlights trade-offs: lightweight NNs suit fast inference, while PINN/SuperNet offer higher accuracy and parameter flexibility.

\subsection*{Limitations and Future Work}
Limitations include moderate parameter/grid ranges ($n_x \le 200$), CPU-only tests, and limited hyperparameter tuning. Future work: scale to GPU, study randomness effects, explore adaptive loss/optimization for PINNs and compare with adaptive numerical methods.
