\begin{abstract}
This report documents the execution of a project series focusing on modeling and simulation of nonlinear dynamical systems described by partial (PDE) and ordinary (ODE) differential equations. The work covers the full spectrum of modeling: from classical numerical discretization and stability analysis (Task 2), through parameter sensitivity analysis (Task 4) and data assimilation (Task 5), to advanced physics-informed machine learning methods (Task 6) and the SuperNet concept (Task 7). Performance measurements were also carried out (Task 3). Results indicate high effectiveness of PINNs in approximating solutions with a limited number of measurement points and show the benefits of ensemble-style approaches (SuperNet) when modeling systems with uncertain parameters.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Problem Statement}
Modeling complex systems in biology or chemistry often leads to reaction-diffusion equations that combine local kinetics (reaction) with spatial mass transport (diffusion).

\subsection{Mathematical Model}
The main object of study in the project is a one-dimensional parabolic equation:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = D \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + R(u), \quad x \in [0, 1], \ t \in [0, T]
\end{equation}
where $u(x,t)$ represents a density or concentration of a substance. The reaction term $R(u)$ is nonlinear and given by:
\begin{equation}
    R(u) = (p + q u)(1 - u)
\end{equation}
Parameters $D, p, q$ control diffusion strength, the baseline growth rate, and the nonlinear autocatalytic effect, respectively.

\subsection{Boundary and Initial Conditions}
To ensure uniqueness of the solution and to model an isolated system, homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions were used:
\begin{equation}
    \left. \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \right|_{x=0} = 0, \quad \left. \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \right|_{x=1} = 0
\end{equation}
The initial condition $u(x,0)$ was specified in three variants:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{gaussian}: a Gaussian profile centered at $0.5$ (modeling a local seed),
    \item \texttt{localized}: a rectangular pulse in the center of the domain,
    \item \texttt{small\_random}: small-amplitude random perturbations to test stability of the steady state.
\end{itemize}

\section{Task 2: Classical Numerical Methods}
\subsection{Spatial Discretization}
The Method of Lines was applied. The spatial domain was divided into $N_x$ points with step $\Delta x = 1/(N_x-1)$. The Laplace operator was approximated by a second-order finite difference:
\begin{equation}
    u_{xx} \approx \frac{u_{i-1} - 2u_i + u_{i+1}}{\Delta x^2}
\end{equation}
Neumann boundary conditions were implemented by modifying the operator matrix (reflecting boundary values), which leads to a sparse tridiagonal matrix. The code uses the \texttt{scipy.sparse.csr\_matrix} format for computational efficiency.

\subsection{Time Integration Methods}
Three schemes with different characteristics were implemented:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Explicit (FE)}: Forward Euler scheme. Simple to implement but limited by the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) stability condition: $\Delta t \le \frac{\Delta x^2}{2D}$. For fine grids this requires very small time steps.
    \item \textbf{Semi-implicit}: Diffusion is treated implicitly (Backward Euler) while reaction is explicit. This avoids the stiff time-step restriction from diffusion. It requires solving a linear system $(I - \Delta t D A)u^{n+1} = u^n + \Delta t R(u^n)$.
    \item \textbf{Crank–Nicolson (CN)}: Second-order accurate in time. Uses the average of explicit and implicit steps for diffusion. It is unconditionally stable for linear problems.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Task 3: Inference and NN Models}
Comparative tests of prediction time were performed. The ODE model, despite its simplicity, requires numerical integration (e.g., RK45), which adds overhead (\SI{1.29}{ms}). A simple feed-forward neural network predicting the next state in one pass achieves \SI{0.87}{ms}, representing about 30\% savings. This suggests potential of machine learning methods for real-time control systems.

\section{Task 4: Sensitivity Analysis (SA)}
An analysis of the impact of parameters $D, p, q$ on system behavior was conducted.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Morris method}: A fast screening method. It revealed that parameter $q$ (autocatalysis) has the most nonlinear and dominant influence on the final profile $u(x,T)$.
    \item \textbf{Sobol analysis}: A variance-based decomposition method. It computed first-order indices and confirmed that $D$ is responsible for smoothing the profile, while interactions between $p$ and $q$ determine the system's bifurcation point.
\end{itemize}

\section{Task 5: Data Assimilation (DA)}
This task concerned reconstructing the system state from noisy measurements.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ABC (Approximate Bayesian Computation)}: A likelihood-free method. It enabled estimation of posterior distributions for $p$ and $q$. The method is stable but computationally expensive (requires thousands of simulations).
    \item \textbf{3D-Var}: A variational method minimizing a cost functional combining deviation from measurements and from the physical operator. 3D-Var proved much faster than ABC, achieving good field prediction $u(x,t)$ even with high noise (SNR < 10dB).
\end{itemize}

\section{Task 6: PINN -- Physics-Informed Neural Networks}
\subsection{Architecture and Training Procedure}
The PINN model was designed as an MLP with 5 layers (64 neurons each) and residual connections (Residual Blocks) every two layers. The \texttt{Tanh} activation was used to ensure continuity of high-order derivatives needed for computing the PDE residual.

\subsection{Loss Function}
The key component of a PINN is a composite loss function:
\begin{equation}
    L = L_{data} + \lambda_{phy} L_{phy} + \lambda_{bc} L_{bc}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $L_{data} = \text{MSE}$ on sparse measurement points (data assimilation).
    \item $L_{phy} = \frac{1}{N_c} \sum |u_t - D u_{xx} - R(u)|^2$ computed at collocation points using \texttt{autograd}.
    \item $L_{bc}$ enforces Neumann boundary conditions.
\end{itemize}
A \textbf{Dynamic Weight Scheduling} was introduced: $\lambda_{phy}$ increases during training (from $0.1$ to $2.0$), allowing the network to first learn the general data shape and then precisely fit the physics.

\section{Task 7: SuperNet and Ensemble Modeling}
\subsection{SuperModel Concept}
In Task 7 we considered a system where physical parameters are known with some error or come from different sources. The SuperModel ODE consists of an ensemble of models coupled by a coupling term:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{u}_i = f(u_i; \theta_i) + C(\bar{u} - u_i)
\end{equation}
where $\bar{u}$ is the ensemble mean. Such synchronization allows more stable prediction than any model alone.

\subsection{SuperNet PINN}
A \textbf{SuperNet PINN} was implemented to learn to represent the entire family of solutions. The network shows strong robustness to input parameter perturbations due to its ability to capture the general structure of the differential operator.

\section{Discussion}
Key observations:
\begin{itemize}
\item Explicit methods are very fast for small grid sizes, but in practice their stability constrains the time step (CFL) — in experiments the explicit method yielded the shortest average time.
\item Implicit schemes (CN and semi-implicit) are slower but more stable and allow larger time steps without loss of stability.
\item PINNs achieved reasonable accuracy (MAE~0.03), which is competitive with classical solvers for certain tasks; however, their training cost is higher than simple PDE solvers.
\item SuperNet PINN reduces average prediction error compared to a single perturbed ODE model, showing that parametric learning can increase robustness and accuracy.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}
The project demonstrated practical differences between numerical approaches and machine learning methods in the context of differential equations. For tasks requiring fast inference it is worth considering lightweight NN models, while for high accuracy and flexible parameter modeling, PINN / SuperNet approaches are advisable.

\subsection*{Limitations and Future Work}
The experiments have several limitations: parameter ranges and grid sizes were moderate (nx up to 200), tests were performed on CPU, and hyperparameter optimization (e.g., architecture, regularization coefficients) was only performed to a limited extent. Future work could include: scaling experiments on GPU, thorough studies of seed and randomness effects, application of advanced optimization techniques for PINNs (adaptive loss weights, larger datasets), and comparison with classical adaptive numerical methods (e.g., adaptive spatio-temporal meshes).
